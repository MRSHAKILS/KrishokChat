Smallholder agriculture in Bangladesh supports over 16 million farming households, where timely access to accurate agronomic advisory is a critical determinant of crop yield, economic security, and public health. Agricultural extension services in the country face severe structural bottlenecks: the ratio of agricultural extension officers to farming households routinely exceeds 1:1,000, creating reliance on informal peer networks, pesticide retailers, and centralized call facilities such as the national Krishi Call Center (16123). In response to these coverage constraints, automated advisory systems powered by Large Language Models (LLMs) and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) have emerged as promising instruments for scalable agricultural knowledge dissemination.

However, deploying generative language models in smallholder advisory involves acute, safety-critical hazards. In agricultural domain advisory—particularly chemical pest and disease management—dosage-bearing advice is safe if and only if *every semantic relation* in the generated output is rigorously supported by an authorized agricultural corpus. An advisory statement that recommends an approved chemical (e.g., mancozeb for potato late blight) but misstates the application concentration (e.g., recommending 20 g/L instead of 2.0 g/L), omits the required spray interval, ignores the pre-harvest interval (PHI), or applies a recommended dosage to the wrong crop stage can cause catastrophic crop loss, environmental contamination, acute operator poisoning, or illegal chemical residues in the food supply.

Existing agricultural RAG architectures and deployed advisory systems rely predominantly on generative prompting or uncalibrated surface-level string matching to verify factual fidelity. As empirical audits demonstrate, substring-level dosage matching cannot verify relational binding: a system may verify the presence of "2 g/L" and "mancozeb" within a retrieved source node while remaining completely blind to the fact that the generated advice paired the concentration with an incompatible crop, an unverified solvent ratio, or a legally restricted chemical. Furthermore, generative models fine-tuned on standard domain QA benchmarks exhibit a persistent chemical hallucination floor (4.05%–7.00%) even when gold reference context is provided, with safety compliance collapsing when models optimize solely for fluency.

This paper addresses this fundamental safety gap by investigating **evidence-linked, relation-aware selective certification for Bengali agricultural advice**. We define selective certification as a calibrated abstention framework wherein an advisory system certifies an output only when its constituent factual relations are provably entailed by retrieved evidence spans from official agricultural authorities, refusing unverified claims with zero tolerance for safety degradation. 

Our investigation focuses on two core challenges:
1. **Gap A (Structured Relational Verification & Calibrated Abstention):** Developing typed evidence relations and development-calibrated selective certification under a transparent retrieval runtime, moving beyond brittle surface string matching.
2. **Gap B (Safety-Constrained Dialect & Script Robustness):** Evaluating dialectal and romanized (Banglish) query variations under a dual-objective protocol that measures retrieval gain alongside strict safety-intent preservation.

### Contributions

To resolve these challenges, we make four contributions:
1. **ClaimSafe-BN:** A frozen, provenance-grounded Bengali agrochemical evaluation benchmark linking atomic claim fields (chemical active, crop, disease/pest, dosage, unit, denominator, interval, PHI, formulation, and applicability predicates) to stable repository node identifiers and exact evidence spans from Bangladesh agricultural research institutes (BARI, BRRI, DAE).
2. **Deterministic-First Structured Relation Verifier:** A relation-aware verification engine that parses generated advisory claims into typed relational schemas and evaluates them against extracted evidence spans, operating under a fail-closed policy.
3. **Calibrated Selective Certification Policy:** A risk-coverage abstention policy calibrated exclusively on development partitions that bounds dangerous non-abstention under strict cost constraints.
4. **Safety-Constrained Dialect Normalization Protocol:** A paired evaluation protocol across standard Bengali, regional dialectal variants (e.g., Chittagong, Sylhet, Noakhali), and romanized Banglish, evaluating retrieval benefit under an explicit non-inferiority constraint on safety-rule adherence.

In accordance with our claim boundaries, we explicitly refrain from claiming multi-agent novelty, visual bounding-box localization, or unsubstantiated longitudinal farmer economic outcomes. The contribution of this work resides strictly in the empirical formulation, measurement, and validation of relation-aware selective certification for low-resource agricultural safety.
