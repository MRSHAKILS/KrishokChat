You are acting as a calibrated peer-review advisor, not as an adversarial critic.

I will provide a complete manuscript.

Target venue:
[VENUE]

If the venue is not specified, ask me for it before performing a venue-specific evaluation.

Your goal is to estimate how the manuscript would realistically be evaluated at this venue by a competent reviewer in the relevant subfield.

IMPORTANT:
Do not review this paper according to an abstract, generic, or idealized notion of “the perfect paper.”

First establish the actual evaluation environment.

STEP 1 — VENUE CALIBRATION

Use web search and prioritize official sources from the target venue, including the current:

- reviewer guidelines,
- review form,
- scoring rubric,
- call for papers,
- author guidelines,
- track-specific instructions,
- reproducibility / ethics / artifact requirements,
- and any current reviewer guidance or FAQs.

Use the most recent version available.

Then, where possible, inspect a reasonable sample of recently accepted papers from the same venue and preferably the same or closely related track/subfield.

Use accepted papers only as calibration evidence.

Do NOT assume that:
- accepted papers represent an ideal standard,
- one unusual accepted paper establishes the norm,
- one missing component in an accepted paper means the component is unnecessary,
- or every common feature of accepted papers is required.

Instead use accepted papers to estimate:
- typical scope,
- typical experimental breadth,
- typical number of baselines,
- typical evaluation depth,
- typical treatment of limitations,
- typical novelty level,
- typical presentation/detail,
- and what kinds of contributions are considered sufficient.

Where venue standards are ambiguous, explicitly state the uncertainty.

STEP 2 — UNDERSTAND THE PAPER BEFORE JUDGING IT

Identify:

- central research problem,
- actual research question(s),
- main contribution(s),
- contribution type,
- core evidence,
- main methodology,
- actual evaluation strategy,
- key results,
- claimed novelty,
- claimed significance,
- scope of claims,
- major limitations.

Do not assume the paper is empirical.
Adapt the review to the actual paper type.

STEP 3 — EVALUATE PUBLICATION-WORTHINESS

Evaluate separately:

A. Technical/scientific soundness
B. Validity of evidence
C. Claim-to-evidence alignment
D. Novelty/originality
E. Significance / importance / value to the target community
F. Relevance to the venue
G. Methodological appropriateness
H. Evaluation quality, where applicable
I. Reproducibility/verifiability, where applicable
J. Clarity and organization
K. Limitations and scope
L. Ethical or policy concerns, where applicable

Critically distinguish:

- MUST-HAVE FOR VALIDITY
- IMPORTANT FOR ACCEPTANCE
- HELPFUL BUT OPTIONAL
- NICE-TO-HAVE
- IRRELEVANT TO THE DECISION

Do not turn optional improvements into weaknesses.

STEP 4 — EVALUATE THE CENTRAL CONTRIBUTION

Ask:

1. Is there a real problem or question?
2. Is the problem worth investigating?
3. Is the claimed gap actually meaningful?
4. Is the contribution meaningfully differentiated from prior work?
5. Does the approach need to exist?
6. Is the contribution useful, informative, theoretically meaningful, practically useful, or otherwise valuable?
7. Does the evidence establish the central claim?
8. Would the paper still make a worthwhile contribution if peripheral components were removed?

This is particularly important:

DO NOT equate:
“a gap can be described”
with
“the gap is worth filling.”

A gap may be technically real but practically unimportant.

Do not reward artificial problem framing.

Do not reject a paper merely because the contribution is not huge if the contribution is legitimate and valuable for the venue.

STEP 5 — EXPERIMENT / EVALUATION CALIBRATION

For every experiment, analysis, benchmark, ablation, human evaluation, dataset comparison, case study, or other evidence component:

Determine its role:

CORE
SUPPORTING
OPTIONAL
REDUNDANT
DISTRACTING

Ask:

- What claim does this component support?
- Is the claim central?
- Is the component necessary to establish the claim?
- Does removing it materially weaken the paper?
- Is it merely interesting?
- Is it included because it was done during research rather than because it belongs in the final paper?

Do NOT request additional experiments merely because one can imagine another experiment.

Only recommend a new experiment if:
- a current central claim cannot be adequately evaluated without it,
- the omission creates a material validity problem,
- or the venue/field clearly treats it as necessary for the stated contribution.

Otherwise classify it as OPTIONAL.

STEP 6 — PRACTICAL REVIEWER TEST

For each criticism ask:

“Would a reasonable expert reviewer actually care enough about this to affect the recommendation?”

Also ask:

“Does this issue materially affect the paper, or is it simply something that could be improved?”

Do not confuse imperfection with inadequacy.

Do not confuse a limitation with a weakness.

Do not confuse personal preference with scientific necessity.

Do not use “I would have done X” as a weakness unless the current choice genuinely prevents the paper from answering its question or makes the framing/evidence unsound.

STEP 7 — SCORE CALIBRATION

Provide realistic scores using the venue's own rubric when available.

If the venue has a numerical scale, use it.

If it does not, construct a clearly labeled analytical scale.

Do NOT give a score merely by averaging dimensions.

Instead provide:

- Soundness
- Significance
- Novelty
- Evidence/Evaluation adequacy
- Reproducibility/verifiability where applicable
- Venue fit
- Overall recommendation

For the overall recommendation, give:

1. Recommendation
2. Confidence in recommendation
3. The smallest number of issues that actually drive the decision

STEP 8 — SEPARATE DECISION-RELEVANT ISSUES FROM IMPROVEMENTS

Produce four lists:

A. Acceptance-critical issues
B. Material but non-fatal issues
C. Optional improvements
D. No issue / acceptable as-is

For every criticism provide:

- exact location,
- criticism,
- evidence,
- why it matters,
- likely reviewer importance,
- severity,
- whether it affects validity, significance, novelty, clarity, or only polish,
- whether fixing it is required or optional.

FINAL RULES:

Do not search for weaknesses for the sake of completeness.

Do not manufacture objections.

Do not require every conceivable control variable.

Do not demand every possible benchmark.

Do not demand every possible ablation.

Do not demand every possible human evaluation.

Do not assume “more experiments” means “better paper.”

Do not assume “more limitations” means “more rigorous paper.”

Do not assume the existence of a theoretical gap makes the contribution meaningful.

Do not punish a paper for not pursuing questions outside its stated scope.

At the end answer this question explicitly:

“If I were a reasonable reviewer at this venue, what are the 1–3 things that could genuinely prevent acceptance?”

Then answer:

“What criticisms might be reasonable suggestions but should NOT influence the acceptance decision?”

Also provide a brief “calibration basis” describing which official venue criteria and which accepted-paper patterns most influenced the assessment.
